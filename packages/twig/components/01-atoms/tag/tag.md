A tag is a short label – usually one to three words – that classifies a page along an axis other than its title. Tags appear most often in page banners. There they signal status ('Mandatory', 'Draft'), audience or scope ('Whole-of-government'), document type ('Plan', 'Communique', 'Case study'), or a temporal horizon ('2025–2027').

Tags carry information that travels with screenshots and search-result snippets. A reader who sees only the banner of a rule page should still know whether the rule is mandatory.

## When to use

Use a tag in a page banner when:

- the page is a rule and its mandatory status needs to be visible
- the page is one of a numbered set and its position in a second classification axis matters (for example, a statement's lifecycle stage)
- the page is a published artefact whose document type is not obvious from the title (a case study, a communique, a plan)
- the page carries a date or a version, and that information forms part of its identity

A single page may carry one tag or a tag-list of two to four. More than four tags in a banner signals an unclear page identity. The tags are then doing the work the title should do.

## When not to use

Do not use a tag for:

- topical taxonomy ('AI', 'investment') – that belongs in the site's content tagging system, not the page banner
- the section the page lives in – breadcrumb covers that
- a call to action – that belongs in a [callout](/components/callout/) or [next step](/components/next-step/)

Section landing pages do not typically carry banner tags – they are navigation surfaces, not classified artefacts.

## Tag-list

A tag-list is a horizontal group of two or more tags shown together. Use a tag-list when a page sits at the intersection of two or more classification axes. An example is a statement that carries both 'Mandatory' and 'Lifecycle: Design'.

Order the tags in the list from most general to most specific: status before audience before topic before date.

## Accessibility

- A tag renders as text, so a screen reader reads it out with the rest of the banner (WCAG 1.3.1 Info and Relationships).
- Colour alone must not separate one tag from another. The words carry the meaning (WCAG 1.4.1 Use of Colour).
- Tag text must make sense away from the page. Readers meet tags in search results and screenshots.
